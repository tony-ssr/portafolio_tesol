Who I Am

About Me

My name is María Alejandra Acosta. I am 21 years old and currently in my seventh semester of the Bachelor's Degree in Spanish and English Teaching. My passion for becoming an educator comes from a very personal place: I want to transform the way languages are experienced in the classroom. Looking back at my own journey, I often felt the limitations of rigid, traditional methods, and that is why my purpose as a future teacher is to ensure my students have far better, warmer, and more inspiring experiences learning Spanish and English than the ones I had. I want to build a space where they feel truly supported, confident, and excited to discover their own voices through language.

🌐

My Vision of Language

For me, language is far more than a set of abstract grammar rules and isolated vocabulary lists; it is a living, breathing bridge of human connection, culture, and identity. Words carry our personal stories, emotions, and worldview — an active social practice belonging to the real world.

✨

My Vision of Language Learning

Learning a language is an exploratory, deeply human journey. I believe learning truly flourishes when students feel emotionally safe, curious, and actively engaged — daring to take risks, making mistakes as natural stepping stones, and discovering one's voice through meaningful interaction.

🏫

My Vision of Language Teaching

My role as a teacher is to be a supportive guide, an attentive listener, and an architect of meaningful communicative experiences. I create low-anxiety spaces where every voice matters, honoring learners' diverse backgrounds and empowering them to use language as a transformative tool.

My Reflections

Reflection About Curriculum

In Colombia, when we hear the word "curriculum," it is almost automatic to think of official guidelines from the Ministry of Education — the Estándares Básicos, the DBAs, or the constant pressure of preparing students for the Saber 11 exam. Viewed that way, it is easy to treat it like a strict checklist we just have to get through before the school year ends. In reality, though, curriculum is much more than a national syllabus. It covers the topics we plan to teach, of course, but it also lives in everything students experience at school: whether they feel comfortable making mistakes, how much room they have to express their ideas, and whether class actually connects to their daily lives.

Given how diverse Colombia is, no document written in an office in Bogotá can predict what a specific group of students needs. A classroom in a rural area with limited resources does not look like a private school in a major city, and neither shares the exact same challenges as a crowded public school. That is why teachers shouldn't follow the curriculum like a rigid script. Approaching it well means treating it as a flexible guide — adapting national objectives to our students' local culture, realities, and pace.

It also means reading those guidelines with a critical eye instead of accepting them blindly. When teachers pause to question whose realities are represented, what gets left out, and whether we are truly helping students think for themselves beyond a test score, the whole classroom changes. In the Colombian context, a curriculum only makes sense when teachers bridge the gap between policy and real life with empathy, common sense, and flexibility.

Workshop Design

The Syllabus

Creating this syllabus for our TESOL workshop honestly changed how I look at course planning. Working with 24 fifth-graders at a rural school in San Andrés de Sotavento, Córdoba, showed my group that good planning is much deeper than an organized list of grammar topics — it demands empathy, flexibility, and a deep understanding of who your learners truly are.

Getting a 4.0 and reading the teacher's comments taught me just as much as writing the syllabus itself. This activity reminded me that good teaching takes both structure and empathy — a syllabus needs clear details on paper, but it only truly works when it is built around real students. Planning Practice

Lesson Plan

While a syllabus gives you the big picture, planning a single class forces you to think through how learning actually happens step by step. Our aim was for students to be better able to create simple sentences using the verb to be in the present simple to describe animals, objects, and people.

Keeping our learners' reality in mind guided every decision we made. Because these students have very limited exposure to English and often feel anxious when asked to speak individually, a traditional, rule-heavy grammar explanation simply would not work. This activity helped me see how closely a syllabus and a lesson plan depend on each other — and how good lesson planning is about building a comfortable, engaging path where students feel confident enough to use the language themselves.

Creative Resources

Teaching Materials

This activity challenged us to design a lesson focused on listening and grammar to teach daily routines, allowing full creative freedom in our pedagogical design. Instead of falling back on standard textbook audios or predictable exercises, we relied on our own creative approach to build materials from scratch — custom worksheets around clips from movies and TV series.

Creating the worksheets ourselves completely changed how I thought about the lesson. We had to carefully choose scenes that matched the students' level and figure out how to guide them smoothly from understanding the audio to noticing and using the target grammar naturally. It showed me how rewarding it is to take creative ownership as a teacher and design original resources that genuinely connect with students.

Looking Back

Portfolio Reflection

Putting this portfolio together has given me a much clearer picture of what it truly means to plan and teach a language. Moving from reflecting on the curriculum in our context to designing a syllabus, building lesson plans, and creating original materials showed me how closely every teaching decision is connected.

What stood out most across all these experiences is that thoughtful planning is never just about organizing grammar points or meeting standards on paper; it is about keeping real students — their backgrounds, and how they feel in the classroom — at the center of the process.
