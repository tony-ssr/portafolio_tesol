Who I Am

About Me

My name is María Alejandra Acosta. I am 21 years old and currently in my seventh semester of the Bachelor's Degree in Spanish and English Teaching. My passion for becoming an educator comes from a very personal place: I want to transform the way languages are experienced in the classroom. Looking back at my own journey, I often felt the limitations of rigid, traditional methods, and that is why my purpose as a future teacher is to ensure my students have far better, warmer, and more inspiring experiences learning Spanish and English than the ones I had. I want to build a space where they feel truly supported, confident, and excited to discover their own voices through language.

🌐

My Vision of Language

For me, language is far more than a set of abstract grammar rules and isolated vocabulary lists; it is a living, breathing bridge of human connection, culture, and identity. In my view, words carry our personal stories, emotions, and worldview. I see language as an active social practice that belongs to the real world, where the true purpose is not achieving sterile perfection, but negotiating meaning, expressing our inner voice, and building deep, empathetic relationships with others.

✨

My Vision of Language Learning

In my perspective, learning a language is an exploratory, deeply human journey rather than a mechanical process of memorization. I believe learning truly flourishes when students feel emotionally safe, curious, and actively engaged with their whole self mind, body, and personal experience. For me, acquiring a language means daring to take risks, making mistakes as natural stepping stones, and discovering one's voice through meaningful interaction and collaborative problem-solving with peers.

🏫

My Vision of Language Teaching

My role as a teacher is to be a supportive guide, an attentive listener, and an architect of meaningful communicative experiences. In my teaching practice, I do not see myself as an authoritarian transmitter of knowledge, but as someone who provides thoughtful scaffolding and creates a low-anxiety space where every voice matters. My commitment is to honor my learners' diverse backgrounds, adapt to their local realities, and empower them to use language as a transformative tool for self-expression and critical thought.

My Reflections

Reflection About Curriculum

In Colombia, when we hear the word "curriculum," it is almost automatic to think of official guidelines from the Ministry of Education, the Estándares Básicos, the DBAs, or the constant pressure of preparing students for the Saber 11 exam. Viewed that way, it is easy to treat it like a strict checklist we just have to get through before the school year ends. In reality, though, curriculum is much more than a national syllabus. It covers the topics we plan to teach, of course, but it also lives in everything students experience at school: whether they feel comfortable making mistakes, how much room they have to express their ideas, and whether class actually connects to their daily lives.

Given how diverse Colombia is, no document written in an office in Bogotá can predict what a specific group of students needs. A classroom in a rural area with limited resources does not look like a private school in a major city, and neither shares the exact same challenges as a crowded public school. That is why teachers shouldn't follow the curriculum like a rigid script. Approaching it well means treating it as a flexible guide-adapting national objectives to our students' local culture, realities, and pace. Sometimes doing our job right means putting the textbook aside for a moment or slowing down when an activity looks great on paper but simply doesn't work in the room.

It also means reading those guidelines with a critical eye instead of accepting them blindly. When teachers pause to question whose realities are represented, what gets left out, and whether we are truly helping students think for themselves beyond a test score, the whole classroom changes. In the Colombian context, a curriculum only makes sense when teachers bridge the gap between policy and real life with empathy, common sense, and flexibility.

Workshop Design

The Syllabus

Creating this syllabus for our TESOL workshop honestly changed how I look at course planning. Before doing it, I tended to think of a syllabus mostly as an organized list of grammar topics. Working with the case of 24 fifth-graders at a rural school in San Andrés de Sotavento, Córdoba, quickly showed my group that it is much deeper than that. Connecting the DBAs and Estándares Básicos with pre-A1 to A1 learners who have strong roots in the Zenú community took real thought. Planning each week also made me think more carefully about how students actually feel in class. Knowing that many of them get nervous speaking English on their own, yet enjoy drawing, crafts, storytelling, and group games, guided almost everything we designed.

Instead of relying on dry exercises, we built topics like family, greetings, pronouns, adjectives, colors, and the verb to be around hands-on, collaborative tasks like: making a relationship tree, role-playing, movement games, and describing their own toys. Getting a 4.0 and reading the teacher's comments taught me just as much as writing the syllabus. The feedback pointed out small things that are easy to miss, like using accurate terms (course or subject instead of assignature), explaining more clearly how using Spanish instructions works alongside TBL, and making sure every percentage is filled in on the evaluation table. In the end, this activity reminded me that good teaching takes both structure and empathy: a syllabus needs clear details on paper, but it only truly works when it is built around real students.

Planning Practice

Lesson Plan

While a syllabus gives you the big picture, planning a single class forces you to think through how learning actually happens step by step. Our aim was for students to be better able to create simple sentences using the verb to be in the present simple to describe animals, objects, and people. Even though the verb to be feels basic to us, putting ourselves in the shoes of pre-A1 to A1 fifth-graders at a rural school made me realize how carefully we needed to scaffold each stage so they would not feel overwhelmed.

Keeping our learners' reality from the syllabus in mind guided every decision we made. Because these students have very limited exposure to English and often feel anxious when asked to speak individually, a traditional, rule-heavy grammar explanation simply would not work. Instead, we focused on making the structure approachable through familiar examples, visual support, and collaboration. Tying this lesson to the bigger goal of describing people, animals, and places from their own community made the grammar feel purposeful rather than disconnected.

In the end, this activity helped me see how closely a syllabus and a lesson plan depend on each other. Having one clear aim kept us from trying to pack too much into a single class and forced us to check whether every task genuinely helped students create their own sentences. Above all, it reminded me that good lesson planning is not just about explaining a grammar point correctly; it is about building a comfortable, engaging path where students feel confident enough to use the language themselves.

Professional Development

Certificate of Attendance

Attending the VI International Conference on Professional Development & The Language Classroom Conference, was a genuinely motivating experience in my teacher training. Being part of this event, especially during the department's 60th-anniversary celebration, gave me the chance to step outside my regular university courses for a few days and look at language teaching from a wider, more real-world perspective.

Beyond picking up new ideas about language and pedagogy, what I valued most was hearing honest classroom experiences from other teachers and researchers. Many of the sessions reinforced something I care deeply about: teaching a language is never just about covering grammar rules. It is about reading our students' contexts with empathy, questioning our own habits in the classroom, and building spaces where learners feel comfortable participating. Getting a closer look at ongoing research and postgraduate opportunities, such as the Master's program in teaching English and Spanish as foreign languages, also reminded me that growing as a teacher does not end at graduation.

Creative Resources

Teaching Materials

This activity challenged us to design a lesson focused on listening and grammar to teach daily routines, allowing full creative freedom in our pedagogical design. Instead of falling back on standard textbook audios or predictable exercises, we relied on our own creative approach to build our materials from scratch. We designed custom worksheets around clips from movies and TV series, aiming to present everyday habits in a way that felt more authentic, visual, and entertaining for learners.

Creating the worksheets ourselves completely changed how I thought about the lesson. We had to carefully choose scenes that matched the students' level and figure out how to guide them smoothly from understanding the audio to noticing and using the target grammar naturally. Even though building everything from zero took extra time and effort, it made the class feel much more dynamic and personal. Above all, it showed me how rewarding it is to take creative ownership as a teacher and design original resources that genuinely connect with students.

Looking Back

Putting this portfolio together has given me a much clearer picture of what it truly means to plan and teach a language.
